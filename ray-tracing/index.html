Light, traced backwards.

Ray tracing draws a picture by asking one question per pixel: if a line left the camera through this pixel, what would it hit, and how much light reaches that spot? This explorer runs a real path tracer in your GPU's shader cores, walks through the algorithm in 3D one step at a time, traces its 60-year history, and shows how modern video cards wire it into silicon.

A path tracer in your shader cores

Every frame, the fragment shader fires one or more rays through each pixel, bounces them around four spheres (mirror, diffuse, glass, brushed gold) and a checker floor, and averages the result into the previous frames. Part 2 · The algorithm

One pixel, step by step

Rasterization asks "which pixels does this triangle cover?" Ray tracing flips it: "what does this pixel see?" The rest is geometry. Step through a single pixel below; drag the view to look at the rays from any angle, and change the pixel to see the same logic play out against a different part of the scene.

for each pixel (x, y): image[x,y] = trace(camera.rayThrough(x, y))

def trace(ray, depth):
    hit = scene.closestHit(ray)          # smallest t over all objects
    if no hit: return sky
    if hit.mirror: return 0.85 · trace(reflect(ray, n), depth+1)
    if hit.glass:  return (1−F) · trace(refract(ray, n), depth+1)
                        +   F   · trace(reflect(ray, n), depth+1)
    color = ambient                      # matte surface: gather light
    if visible(hit.p, light):            # shadow ray
        color += albedo · max(0, n·l)
    return color                         # unwinds back up to the pixel

    How the ray gets around

    Numbers in the 3D view match the numbered sentences above. At every surface the ray meets, the material decides what happens next, and the renderer handles it by calling the same trace() function again with a new ray, which is the recursion that made Whitted's method famous:

    Mirror. The ray bounces off with the angle of incidence equal to the angle of reflection, r = d − 2(d·n)n. Whatever the reflected ray sees, scaled by the mirror's reflectivity (0.85 here), becomes this pixel's color.

    Glass. Snell's law bends the ray: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. Entering a denser medium (air → glass, n = 1.5) it bends toward the normal; leaving, it bends away again. The ray crosses the inside of the sphere, exits on the far side and carries on, so what you see "in" the glass is really the scene behind it, displaced. A small Fresnel fraction F (about 4% head-on, rising to 100% at grazing angles) reflects off the surface instead, drawn here as a faint pink ray. If the ray reaches the inner wall too obliquely it cannot exit at all and reflects back inside (total internal reflection).

    Matte. A diffuse surface ends the chain. Its brightness depends on whether it can see the light (one shadow ray) and on the angle between its normal and the light direction, max(0, n·l).

    When every recursive call has returned, the colors multiply back down the chain: the matte surface's shaded color is tinted by each glass pass and scaled by each mirror bounce until it reaches the pixel. Press Play to watch the ray extend one segment at a time, or aim the pixel at a different material to compare paths.

    This is Whitted's 1979 recipe. Replace the single, deterministic secondary ray with a random bounce at every surface, average many of them, and you have Kajiya's path tracing, which the live render in Part 1 runs.

    Ray–sphere intersection, the whole thing

    Almost all of ray tracing is this one computation repeated billions of times. A ray is a point and a direction, p(t) = o + t·d. Substituting it into a sphere's equation gives a quadratic in t; the smallest positive root is the nearest hit.

    |o + t·d − c|² = r²
    → t² + 2t·(d·(o−c)) + |o−c|² − r² = 0
    → t = −b ± √(b² − (|o−c|² − r²)), b = d·(o−c)

    Triangles use a similar closed form (Möller–Trumbore). The reason GPUs can now put this in hardware is that it is tiny, fixed, and embarrassingly parallel: every pixel, every bounce, independent of all the others.

    Why it is slow

    A 1080p image is about 2 million pixels. One path with 4 bounces plus a shadow ray at each is ~8 rays, so a single sample per pixel is 16 million rays. Smooth images need tens to thousands of samples, and at 60 fps you multiply by 60 again. Each ray must find its nearest hit among millions of triangles, which is why the acceleration structure (Part 3) matters more than the intersection math itself.

    Part 3 · History

    From a 74-minute image to 60 frames a second

    Ray tracing is older than the GPU. The ideas were mostly settled by 1986; the following thirty years were about making them fast enough. Items marked hardware are the silicon milestones.

    1. 1525
      Dürer's perspective machine

      A string from a fixed point on the wall to a point on the lute, passing through a frame: a physical primary ray. The artist marks where it crosses the frame and gets a perspective drawing. The geometry is identical to a modern camera model.

    2. 1968
      Arthur Appel · ray casting

      Working at IBM, Appel shoots a ray per pixel to find the visible surface and a second ray toward the light to decide if a point is in shadow. No recursion yet. This is "Max bounces = 0" in the render above, plus hard shadows.

    3. 1979
      Turner Whitted · recursive ray tracing

      At Bell Labs, Whitted makes the hit point spawn new rays: one reflected, one refracted, one toward each light. Mirrors, glass and shadows fall out of a single recursive function. His 512×512 test image reportedly took about 74 minutes on a VAX-11/780. The paper is published in 1980.

    4. 1984
      Cook, Porter & Carpenter · distributed ray tracing

      Lucasfilm's graphics group jitters the rays: across a lens for depth of field, across time for motion blur, across an area light for soft shadows, across a cone for glossy reflection. Noise becomes an accepted trade for realism.

    5. 1986
      James Kajiya · the rendering equation and path tracing

      Kajiya writes down the integral every renderer is trying to solve, and proposes solving it by Monte Carlo: follow random paths of light, average. Every "physically based" renderer since is a strategy for sampling this integral with less noise.

    6. 1986–87
      Acceleration structures

      Kay & Kajiya, Goldsmith & Salmon, and Arvo & Kirk turn nearest-hit search from O(N) to O(log N) with bounding-volume hierarchies and spatial subdivision. The BVH wins in the long run and is what RT cores traverse today.

    7. 1996–97
      Photon mapping · bidirectional & Metropolis methods

      Henrik Wann Jensen's photon maps and Eric Veach's thesis (multiple importance sampling, bidirectional path tracing, Metropolis light transport) attack the hard cases: caustics, tiny lights, light arriving through a keyhole.

    8. 2002
      Purcell et al. · ray tracing on programmable GPUs

      Stanford shows a full ray tracer mapped onto the first programmable pixel shaders, treating the GPU as a stream processor. Awkward and slow, but it proves the parallelism fits.

    9. 2005
      Saarland RPU hardware

      Sven Woop, Jörg Schmittler and Philipp Slusallek build the first dedicated ray-tracing processor on an FPGA, with hardware k-d tree traversal. A research prototype, but the blueprint for later RT units.

    10. 2006
      Monster House · first fully path-traced feature film

      Sony Pictures Imageworks renders the whole film with Arnold. Pixar follows with ray-traced global illumination on Monsters University (2013) and Disney with Hyperion on Big Hero 6 (2014). Film settles on path tracing as the default.

    11. 2009–11
      NVIDIA OptiX · Intel Embree

      Two production ray-tracing kernels, one for GPUs and one for CPUs. Both are still the core of many renderers. OptiX introduces the shader-stage model (ray generation, intersection, closest-hit, miss) that DXR later standardizes.

    12. 2018
      DirectX Raytracing · NVIDIA Turing RT cores hardware

      Microsoft announces DXR in March. In September NVIDIA ships GeForce RTX 20 with RT cores, fixed-function units for BVH traversal and ray–triangle tests, quoted at ~10 billion rays/s. Battlefield V is the first game with real-time ray-traced reflections in November.

    13. 2020
      Vulkan RT · AMD RDNA 2 · PS5 and Xbox Series X hardware

      Khronos finalizes cross-vendor Vulkan ray tracing. AMD adds a Ray Accelerator to every compute unit in RX 6000, and the same design ships inside both new consoles, so hardware ray tracing becomes a baseline for games. Cyberpunk 2077 and Minecraft RTX arrive.

    14. 2022
      Ada Lovelace · Intel Arc · RDNA 3 hardware

      Third-generation RT cores add Shader Execution Reordering (grouping divergent rays by material), Opacity Micromaps (alpha-tested leaves without a shader call) and Displaced Micro-Meshes. Intel enters with Arc's Ray Tracing Units. Hardware RT is now three vendors wide.

    15. 2023
      Real-time path tracing in shipped games

      Cyberpunk 2077's Overdrive mode and Alan Wake 2 run full path tracing at a few samples per pixel, made presentable by AI denoisers and ray reconstruction. The 1986 algorithm, at 60 fps, with a lot of help.

    16. 2025
      Blackwell RTX 50 · RDNA 4 hardware

      Fourth-generation RT cores add clustered BVH handling ("Mega Geometry") so ray tracing keeps up with virtualized, per-frame-changing geometry. AMD's third-generation Ray Accelerators widen the tree to 8 children per node and add oriented bounding boxes. Ray tracing reaches handheld consoles.

    Part 4 · Realization in the video card

    What the GPU actually does with a ray

    A modern graphics card does not "ray trace" the way it rasterizes. Most of the work still runs on the ordinary shader cores. What the RT unit adds is a hardware answer to the one question that dominates the cost: which triangle does this ray hit first? Everything else, deciding what to do at the hit, is programmable.

    The ray tracing pipeline (DXR / Vulkan RT / OptiX)

    01 · shader
    Ray generation

    Runs once per pixel (or per sample). Builds the primary ray and calls TraceRay(). Later, accumulates the result.

    02 · RT core
    BVH traversal

    Walks the top-level structure (instances) into bottom-level structures (meshes), testing ray against bounding boxes. Fixed-function, no shader involved.

    03 · RT core
    Ray–triangle test

    At each leaf, intersects the ray with the stored triangles and keeps the nearest. Custom shapes (spheres, curves) fall back to an intersection shader.

    04 · shader
    Any-hit

    Optional. Called for candidate hits to accept or reject them, mostly for alpha-tested foliage. Opacity micromaps move much of this into hardware.

    05 · shader
    Closest-hit / Miss

    Runs once the nearest hit is final. Shades the surface, and may call TraceRay() again for shadows or bounces. Miss returns the sky.

    Why the acceleration structure is the whole game

    A ray must find its nearest hit among millions of triangles, and brute force means testing every one. A bounding-volume hierarchy wraps triangles in boxes, boxes in bigger boxes, and lets the ray skip entire subtrees whose box it misses.     Green boxes were entered, red boxes were rejected with one test. In a real scene with 10 million triangles a ray typically touches a few dozen boxes and a handful of triangles. The RT core holds this traversal loop in silicon: it reads compressed nodes from memory, performs several box tests per clock, and only wakes a shader when a hit is confirmed.

    Inside an RT core

    Box test units evaluate the slab test (ray vs. axis-aligned box) for several children at once; NVIDIA, AMD and Intel all use wide nodes (4 to 8 children) stored in a compressed format so a node fits in one cache line.

    Triangle test units run the ray–triangle intersection and track the current nearest t so later, farther candidates are rejected early.

    A traversal stack lives in dedicated on-chip storage so the shader core isn't juggling it in registers. On AMD RDNA 2/3 the traversal loop itself is still shader code with hardware intersection instructions; RDNA 4 and all NVIDIA generations run the loop in hardware.

    Coherence hardware is the newest piece. Rays leaving neighbouring pixels scatter in different directions and hit different materials, which is poison for SIMD execution. Shader Execution Reordering (Ada and later) sorts live rays by their hit shader before shading so a warp runs one material at a time.

    How a game frame uses it: hybrid rendering

    No shipping game traces every ray. The common frame today is: rasterize the scene into a G-buffer (positions, normals, materials) as before; then trace one or a few rays per pixel for the effects rasterization fakes badly, such as shadows from area lights, reflections of off-screen objects and indirect light. The result is far too noisy to show, so a denoiser, now usually a neural network, reconstructs a clean image from the sparse samples and the G-buffer, and an upscaler fills in resolution. "Path traced" game modes run the same loop with more bounces and lean harder on the denoiser.

    Rough budget for 1440p at 60 fps on a current high-end card: ~3.7 million pixels × 60 frames × 2–4 rays ≈ 0.5–1 billion rays a second, each one traversing a BVH of several million triangles. The live render at the top of this page does the same work with no RT core at all, in plain shader code, which is why it needs a second or two to clear up.
